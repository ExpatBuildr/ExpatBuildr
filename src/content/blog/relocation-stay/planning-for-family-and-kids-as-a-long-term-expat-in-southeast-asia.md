Everything about long-term expat planning changes once kids are part of the picture, and most of the content aimed at this audience — including most of this pillar — is written for a solo founder or a couple without children. Schools, healthcare, and visa documentation all work differently when you're planning for a family, and the costs involved are real enough that they change the underlying math of whether a specific city, or even a specific country, still makes sense.

Here's what actually changes.

## Schools: the decision that shapes almost everything else

**Philippines.** Foreign children can enroll in both public and private schools, but the practical reality diverges sharply between the two. Public schools generally work only for families whose children already speak Filipino or a local language, since most instruction below the international-school tier happens in Filipino, and expat kids without that language foundation tend to fall behind quickly in a system with large class sizes and limited support for non-native speakers. International schools are the realistic path for most expat families, teaching in English or another home-country language with curricula (American, British, IB) that keep continuity with wherever the family might move next.

**Thailand.** The pattern is similar but the public-school door is more explicitly restricted — many Thai public schools have nationality-based restrictions, and where foreign children can attend, they generally pay tuition fees Thai families don't, on top of the same Thai-language instruction barrier. Thailand has an unusually large international school market as a result — over 200 schools offering British, American, and IB curricula, concentrated in Bangkok, with real but more limited options outside major cities. Families in more rural areas of Thailand often end up choosing between boarding options or homeschooling rather than a rural international school.

**The real cost, current as of this year:** Thailand's top-tier international schools run roughly 500,000-1,000,000 THB per year (about $13,500-$27,000 USD), with more moderate options in the 300,000-800,000 THB range ($8,000-$21,500 USD). This is genuinely one of the largest line items in a family relocation budget, and it's worth building into your income-runway planning specifically, not folding into a general "cost of living" estimate that undercounts it.

**What every school, in either country, will ask for:** the child's passport and valid visa, a birth certificate (apostilled if issued abroad, with translation if not in English), and transcripts or a transfer certificate from any previous school. Starting this documentation process early matters more than most families expect — popular international schools in both countries carry waitlists, and admissions can be tied to specific visa timing.

## Healthcare: generally strong, with real planning still required

Both countries offer genuinely good private pediatric care in major cities, with English-speaking staff and costs that remain far below Western equivalents even accounting for real medical inflation (running 10-18% annually in the Philippines as of recent data, which matters if you're budgeting multiple years out rather than just year one).

**Philippines specifically:** the healthcare system splits sharply between an underfunded public sector and a strong private sector, meaning the "good healthcare in the Philippines" reputation is really a private-sector reputation — worth confirming your specific city has private pediatric care you're comfortable with, since quality varies more outside Manila and Cebu. A practical detail worth knowing in advance: confirm your insurance policy includes direct billing with the hospital nearest you, since without it, families can end up needing to pay significant costs upfront before insurance reimbursement.

**Thailand specifically:** private hospital visits can run as low as 1,000 THB (about $27 USD) for a standard visit, with annual family insurance typically in the 40,000-100,000 THB range ($1,100-$2,700 USD) — genuinely affordable relative to Western costs, with the kind of infrastructure (English-speaking pediatricians, established international-standard hospitals) that makes this one of the more straightforward parts of family planning in Thailand specifically.

## Visa and legal status: your family's timeline, not just yours

This is where family planning connects back to the core question this pillar is built around. A solo founder or couple without kids has real flexibility to move fast on a visa decision. A family with kids in school does not — pulling a child mid-year out of an international school, or timing a visa transition around an academic calendar, adds real constraints that don't show up in the general visa-comparison content covered in [Retirement Visa vs Work Visa vs Marriage Visa](/blog/relocation-stay/retirement-visa-vs-work-visa-vs-marriage-visa-philippines).

Practical implications worth building into your planning specifically: most Philippine and Thai schools follow an academic year running roughly August/September through May/June, meaning a mid-year move often means either a partial year of disruption or waiting months to enroll cleanly. If you're weighing a 13(a) marriage-based visa path specifically, note that Philippine citizenship rules treat foreign husbands and foreign wives of Filipino citizens differently — covered in [Marrying a Local: What It Actually Changes Legally](/blog/relocation-stay/marrying-a-local-what-it-actually-changes-legally) — which can matter for how you're planning your own status alongside your children's.

## Where this gets specific to your family

Everything above is the general shape of family planning in both countries. What it can't resolve: which specific city or school actually fits your children's ages, language backgrounds, and academic needs; how to time a visa application around a school year without creating a gap in either; or how the added cost of international school tuition changes the income runway you actually need, on top of everything covered in [how to build income before you move](/blog/relocation-stay/how-to-build-income-before-you-move-to-philippines-or-thailand).
