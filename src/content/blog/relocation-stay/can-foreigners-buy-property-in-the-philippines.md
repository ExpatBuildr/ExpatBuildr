Yes, but only condominiums outright — land ownership is constitutionally off-limits to foreign individuals, with no exceptions for visa type, length of residency, or investment size. That single fact should shape how you think about buying in the Philippines from the start, rather than something you discover mid-negotiation on a house-and-lot property you assumed worked the same way a condo does.

Here's what you can actually own, and what changed as recently as this year.

## The constitutional wall: no land ownership for foreign individuals

Article XII, Section 7 of the 1987 Constitution reserves land ownership for Filipino citizens and corporations that are at least 60% Filipino-owned. This is not a regulation that shifts with policy changes — it's constitutional, and it applies uniformly regardless of what visa you hold, how long you've lived in the Philippines, or how much capital you're bringing in. There are exactly two narrow exceptions: hereditary succession, and specific provisions available to former Filipino citizens. Neither applies to the ordinary foreign buyer this article is written for.

## The real exception: condominiums, owned outright

Under the Condominium Act (Republic Act No. 4726), a condo unit is legally treated as personal property, separate from the land beneath it. The land and common areas belong to the condominium corporation, which itself must remain at least 60% Filipino-owned — but your individual unit is genuinely, fully yours, held the same way a Filipino buyer would hold it.

The one real constraint: foreign ownership within a single building can't exceed 40% of total units. Before committing to a specific building, confirming it hasn't hit that quota is a standard, necessary step your broker or lawyer should verify.

This is why condos dominate foreign real estate investment in the Philippines — Metro Manila's central business districts (Makati, BGC, Ortigas) and provincial destinations like Cebu and Mactan see the bulk of foreign condo purchases, and it's the cleanest, least complicated ownership path available to you.

## What changed in January 2026: the new 99-year lease law

If your information on this topic is more than a year old, this is the update worth knowing. The Investors' Lease Act (Republic Act No. 12252), effective January 2026, extended the maximum land lease term available to qualifying foreign investors up to 99 years, a significant jump from the older 50+25 year residential structure that previously applied.

This doesn't create land ownership — it's still a lease, not a title — but it meaningfully extends how long a foreigner can secure land use through a legal lease arrangement, which matters specifically if you're set on a house-and-lot property rather than a condo. Worth confirming with a property lawyer whether a specific project or purchase actually qualifies as a "qualifying investment project" under the new law, since the 99-year term applies to a defined category, not universally to every lease.

## What this means if you specifically want a house, not a condo

Land ownership being off the table doesn't mean landed property is unavailable to you — it means your paths run through a lease (now potentially up to 99 years under the new law, or the traditional structure for smaller residential purposes), a corporate structure with its own compliance requirements, or, if applicable, arrangements through a Filipino spouse. On that last point specifically: marriage to a Filipino citizen does not grant land ownership either, including for funds you personally contribute — a detail with real financial implications covered in full in [Marrying a Local: What It Actually Changes Legally](/blog/relocation-stay/marrying-a-local-what-it-actually-changes-legally).
