There are four real paths to staying in the Philippines long term: keep extending a tourist visa, get the Digital Nomad Visa, get the SRRV, or get the 13A through marriage. That's it. Everything else you'll read online is a variation, a workaround, or a scam. Pick based on your age, your income source, and whether you're planning to stay indefinitely or just don't want to think about it for a few years.

I didn't know any of this when I moved to Cebu. I picked the path everyone picks by default, tourist extensions, because it was the only thing anyone told me about, and it worked fine for a while. It's still working fine for a lot of people. But "fine for now" and "the right long-term structure for your situation" are two different questions, and nobody answers the second one for free because the honest answer always starts with "it depends."

Here's what it depends on. See every guide in this pillar at the [Relocation & Stay Strategy hub](/blog/relocation-stay).

## Path 1: Tourist visa extensions (the default, not a strategy)

Most people staying long term in the Philippines are on this path, usually without deciding to be. You land visa-free for 30 days, extend once to reach 59 days, then keep extending in monthly or bimonthly increments at any Bureau of Immigration office.

Non-restricted nationalities (most Western passport holders, including Americans) can extend continuously for up to 36 months total before needing to exit and re-enter. Visa-required nationalities cap out around 24 months. Past 59 days, you're required to get an ACR I-Card. Overstay fines run about ₱500 per month, and long overstays can put you on an immigration watchlist. (See [what actually happens if you overstay](/blog/relocation-stay/what-happens-when-your-visa-runs-out-philippines) for the full breakdown.)

**This is not a long-term stay strategy. It's a runway.** It's the easiest path to start on and the worst path to still be on five years from now, because it never converts into anything: no work rights, no path to permanent residency, and every extension is technically discretionary, meaning an immigration officer can, in theory, decline it. In practice this rarely happens if your paperwork and history are clean, but "in practice" is doing a lot of work in that sentence for anyone planning to stay permanently.

Who this actually fits: people who genuinely haven't decided if the Philippines is permanent yet, and want to buy 1-3 years to find out without committing to anything.

## Path 2: The Digital Nomad Visa (new, and probably underused)

This is the newest option and the one most remote-income expats haven't looked into yet, because it didn't exist until Executive Order No. 86 rolled it out in 2025. It's built specifically for people earning foreign-sourced income while living in the Philippines, which, if you're reading this site, is probably you.

Requirements: you're at least 18, you work remotely for clients or employers based outside the Philippines, you can show foreign-sourced income around $24,000 a year, you carry health insurance covering your stay, you have a clean criminal record, and your home country has a reciprocal arrangement with the Philippines. It grants a one-year stay, renewable for a second year, with multiple entry. Processing has reportedly run anywhere from three weeks to three months depending on the embassy.

The tradeoff versus tourist extensions: this is a real application that can be refused, where extensions are close to automatic. The tradeoff in your favor: it's built for exactly the income structure most people in this audience actually have, and it gets you off the extension treadmill without needing $15,000-$50,000 sitting in a bank deposit.

Who this actually fits: remote workers and founders with steady foreign income who want a cleaner legal footing than tourist status, but aren't ready for, or don't qualify for, SRRV or 13A.

## Path 3: SRRV (the deposit-based path)

The Special Resident Retiree's Visa, run by the Philippine Retirement Authority, is the closest thing the Philippines has to "pay your way into permanent residency." Despite the name, you don't need to be retired. Since a September 2025 reform, anyone 40 or older who meets the deposit requirement can apply, and you're free to keep working remotely or running a business the entire time you hold it.

The deposit requirement is tiered by age and pension status:

| Category | Age | Deposit Required |
|---|---|---|
| SRRV Classic, with pension | 50+ | $15,000 |
| SRRV Classic, with pension | 40-49 | $25,000 |
| SRRV Classic, no pension | 50+ | $30,000 |
| SRRV Classic, no pension | 40-49 | $50,000 |
| SRRV Courtesy* | 50+ | $1,500 |

*Courtesy category is restricted to former Filipino citizens, retired diplomats, foreign military, or international-organization officers, not available to most applicants.

The deposit isn't a fee. It's your money, held in a PRA-accredited Philippine bank, and it's refundable if you ever cancel the visa. On top of the deposit there's roughly a $1,500 one-time application fee and $360/year in maintenance fees. What you get in exchange: indefinite residency, multiple-entry privileges, no more extension trips to the BI, no exit clearances.

Who this actually fits: people 40+ with either a documented lifetime pension or enough liquid capital to park in a Philippine bank, who want the certainty of permanent status without needing a Filipino spouse.

## Path 4: 13A marriage visa (the path with no deposit, but the most paperwork)

If you're married to a Filipino citizen, this is usually the better deal financially: there's no bank deposit requirement at all. Your costs are limited to government fees (roughly ₱25,000-₱40,000 across both stages) plus whatever you spend on document prep, apostilles, and legal help if you use it.

The process runs in two stages: a one-year Probationary Visa first, then conversion to Permanent Resident status once that year is up. Once permanent, it doesn't expire and doesn't require annual renewal. It also gives you the clearest path to legal work in the Philippines of any option on this list, though depending on your situation you may still need to coordinate a separate Alien Employment Permit through DOLE.

The catch is the documentation load: apostilled birth certificates, police clearances, marriage certificates, an interview to verify the marriage is genuine, and if you married outside the Philippines, a Report of Marriage filed with the PSA first. None of it is hard. All of it is slow if you don't sequence it correctly, and the interview requirement means BI is actively screening for marriages of convenience, so the standard advice to "just marry a local for the visa" is worse advice than it sounds.

Who this actually fits: people genuinely and legally married to a Filipino citizen. Not a shortcut for anyone else. If you're weighing this against a work visa because you happen to qualify for both, see [Retirement Visa vs Work Visa vs Marriage Visa](/blog/relocation-stay/retirement-visa-vs-work-visa-vs-marriage-visa-philippines) for the direct comparison.

## So which one is actually right for you

Here's the honest answer, and it's the same answer every one of these guides eventually backs into: **it depends on your age, your income structure, your relationship status, and your actual time horizon**, and none of those show up in a generic comparison table. A 34-year-old solo founder earning $60K/year in consulting income has a completely different correct answer than a 52-year-old with a pension and no spouse, or a 41-year-old engaged to a Filipina he hasn't married yet.

That's the actual gap this article can't close, and it's not a content problem, it's a structural one: the right visa strategy is a function of your specific facts, and specific facts don't fit in a blog post written for everyone. If you've read this far, you're probably past the "what are my options" stage and into the "which one is actually right for me, and how do I sequence it correctly" stage.
