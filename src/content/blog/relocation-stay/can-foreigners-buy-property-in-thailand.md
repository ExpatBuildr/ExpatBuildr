Yes, but only condominiums outright — land ownership is off-limits to foreign individuals under a law dating back to 1954, and every "workaround" you'll hear about is either a legitimate, narrower structure with real requirements, or an illegal arrangement that can cost you the property entirely. Understanding the actual legal landscape before you start looking at listings matters more in Thailand than almost anywhere else in the region, because the workarounds here carry real, well-documented enforcement risk.

Here's what you can legally own, and where the genuine risk sits.

## The foundation: no land ownership for foreign individuals

Thailand's Land Code (B.E. 2497, dating to 1954) prohibits foreign nationals from owning land, with no treaty currently in force that changes this for individual buyers. This is not a grey area — it's explicit, longstanding statute, and the type of property you're looking at determines what ownership structure is actually available to you.

## Condominiums: the clean, direct exception

The Condominium Act (B.E. 2522) allows foreigners full freehold ownership of a condo unit, the most straightforward and secure path available. The constraint: foreign ownership is capped at 49% of a building's total registered floor area. Verifying remaining quota, through the building's management company or directly at the Land Department, is a standard step before committing to a purchase.

Entry-level foreign-quota condos in popular markets start around 2.5-3 million THB depending on location. All freehold condo purchases must be funded with money transferred from overseas, documented through a Foreign Exchange Transaction (FET) form — this is a mandatory part of the registration process, not optional paperwork.

## Villas and landed houses: building and land are separated

Thai law treats land and buildings as legally separate assets, which is what makes villa ownership possible at all for foreigners. In practice, this means you can own the physical structure while the land underneath is secured through a registered lease, typically running 30 years, sometimes extended contractually to 30+30 or 30+30+30.

**The caveat worth being precise about:** those multi-term renewal extensions are contractual promises, not guaranteed legal rights. Thai courts have not consistently treated 30+30-style renewal clauses as binding on a landowner's heirs or successors — meaning a "90-year deal" is, in the strictest legal sense, a 30-year lease with an expectation, not a guarantee, of renewal twice more. This matters most if the original landowner passes away or sells during your lease term.

## The nominee company structure: legal in principle, actively enforced against in practice

This is the one worth understanding carefully rather than dismissing or embracing based on secondhand advice. Some foreigners set up a Thai limited company — holding 49% of shares personally, with Thai partners holding the required 51% — allowing the company to purchase land.

This structure is legal when the Thai shareholders are genuine individuals with a real, demonstrable, verifiable source of funds, and the company conducts actual business activity (rental income, property management, something real). It becomes illegal, and this is actively enforced, when the Thai shareholders are nominees in name only, with no genuine stake or involvement — a structure the Land Department has stepped up scrutiny of in recent years, including interviewing Thai shareholders directly and reviewing capital contribution sources. Thai authorities have prosecuted several hundred companies linked to exactly this kind of illicit nominee arrangement.

If a nominee structure is pitched to you as a simple, common workaround, that framing should raise your guard, not lower it. It's a real legal structure that requires real compliance, ongoing annual costs (typically 15,000-30,000 THB for accounting, audit, and tax filings), and genuine legal exposure if it's not done correctly.

## Where this gets specific to you

Whether a condo purchase suits your situation better than pursuing a leasehold villa, how to structure a lease agreement as protectively as current practice allows, and whether a corporate structure genuinely makes sense for your circumstances (it rarely does for a straightforward personal residence) are decisions that depend on what you're actually trying to build.
